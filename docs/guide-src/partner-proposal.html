THE PROBLEM

One question nobody could answer

On 26 August 2026 an ice and rock avalanche from Langtang Lirung sent a flash flood down the Bhote Koshi and Trishuli. Within days the government convened its council, deployed 21,000 security personnel, sent 679,295 warning messages in one minute and restored 116 of 120 damaged telecom towers. Mapping volunteers traced 16,388 buildings from satellite imagery. The response had resources.

What went wrong was not effort. It was information.

  • Relief collected under the one-door system was stockpiled in Bidur while villages in Rasuwa received nothing. Twelve days in, Aamachhodingmo Rural Municipality, home to about 7,000 people, was asking for salt and cooking oil.
  • Federal and local tiers ran separate operations. Ministries used separate online forms that duplicated each other. One Finance Ministry officer built his own tool to merge the missing-person lists from NDRRMA, Police and the Army every five minutes.
  • Drone crews collected requests by loudspeaker, with villagers writing phone numbers in the mud, and kept clear of helicopters by hand.
  • The deputy mayor of Bidur said she could not find people to walk five minutes to unreached spots. Nobody had a list of which settlements had been reached and which had not.

This is not new

"Cases of duplication or under-distribution of aid… teams neglected some isolated areas to cover others, unaware of the efforts already undertaken."European Institute for Asian Studies, lessons from the 2015 earthquake response. More than 300 NGOs worked in Gorkha district alone.
"Relief distribution faced significant discrepancies, leading to conflicts… lack of accurate data on vulnerable populations."DPNet Nepal field assessment after the Jajarkot earthquake, February 2024.

Across three responses in eleven years the same gap appears: the money, goods and people were there, but no shared record showed where they had gone. VerifiedNepal exists to keep that record.

WHAT THE EVIDENCE SAYS

Why most response tools fade, and a few last

Before building anything new, we studied the civic and humanitarian platforms used since Haiti 2010, including Nepal's own QuakeMap from 2015. The pattern is consistent. Tools that survive do one narrow job, keep a visible human verification step, let data leave in standard formats, and have an institution that reads them on a schedule. Tools that try to do everything, or depend on a volunteer team alone, stop being used when the emergency ends.

The lesson we took. Own one record, the ward-level register of what is needed, who has taken it and what was delivered. Verify before publishing. Export in the formats the cluster system already uses. Give a government desk and the Red Cross a feed they can read every hour. Keep the platform in use every monsoon so it is already the habit on the day of the earthquake.

WHERE WE STAND

What VerifiedNepal does today

VerifiedNepal is an independent, volunteer-run, bilingual platform covering all 77 districts. It went live during the 2026 Bhote Koshi flood and now supports any number of disasters at once. It is not a government website and it handles no money. Everything below is running now.

Requests and offers, verified by people

  • Anyone can ask for help without an account, in Nepali or English.
  • A moderator calls to verify before anything is published. Every decision is written to a public audit log.
  • Verified organizations, helper groups and individuals take a published request, see the family's contact privately, and mark it delivered.

Organizations and drop centers

  • Organizations register once, are verified, and carry a public trust tier.
  • Drop centers record every movement of goods in a public ledger anyone can download.
  • A donor can follow one donation from the center to the family.

Works where there is no signal

  • Printed claim sheets with QR codes for field distribution.
  • Codes redeemed on paper are reconciled later in one pass.
  • Names are masked in every public view. Phone numbers are shown only to the verified handler.

Also on the platform

  • Missing-person posters and a search across the official rescued and missing lists.
  • Community rebuild projects with a public spending trail.
  • Field articles and short photo stories, all moderated.

WHAT WE ARE BUILDING NOW

The ward register

Work started in September 2026 and ships in stages to the live site. The first stage answers the Rasuwa question directly.

  1. A municipality and ward code on every record. All 753 municipalities and their wards, taken from the government's BIPAD dataset, so "Gosaikunda ward 3" means the same thing in our data, in yours and in the district office.
  2. A public coverage page per disaster. For every ward: requests open, in progress, delivered, and days since anything was last delivered. Wards that have received nothing sort to the top.
  3. One-click 3W export. A who-does-what-where file with HXL tags and a GeoJSON layer, the formats OCHA information officers and HDX already accept. No retyping into the cluster template.
  4. A delivery receipt. Photo, location and number of households served, attached when a request is marked delivered. The count is public. The photo and location are visible only to moderators and the family.
  5. Requests that come back. A request taken but not delivered within six days returns to the open list automatically, and the family sees each step on their status page. This is the same rule 2,000 organizations follow on Crisis Cleanup, and it is what stops two teams working the same house while a third is missed.

Next stages, in order

  • Offline entry for field teams. Register, receive and deliver with no signal; the phone syncs when a bar appears.
  • One-door distribution filing. An organization files what it will distribute, in which wards, on which date. The palika or district office acknowledges it on the platform, and the record becomes the DDMC daily distribution log the 2017 one-door policy requires.
  • Drone tasking board. Payload requests from wards, a roster of operators with payload and permits, landing sites registered by ward chairs, and a shared timeline of planned flights per district.
  • Staff assignments and shift handover inside the organization dashboard, and requests expressed in standard kit units.

WHO BENEFITS

What each partner gets

International NGOs and UN agencies

  • A 3W you do not have to compile. Your deliveries recorded on the platform export as an HXL-tagged file with province, district, municipality and ward codes. Upload to HDX or hand to the cluster information officer unchanged.
  • Fewer duplicates. One handler per request, expiring claims, and a public register of who is working where. In 2023, 41.6% of cash and voucher practitioners surveyed by CALP named deduplication and data sharing as a top need.
  • Verified local partners. Every organization on the platform has been checked and carries a visible trust tier. Humanitarian ID was decommissioned in January 2026; there is currently no other shared registry at this level.
  • Nothing new for field staff to learn. Paper claim sheets, QR codes and a phone browser. Data leaves in open formats whenever you need it.

Nepali NGOs and community organizations

  • Free, with no IT to run. Register once, invite your team, take requests, mark them delivered.
  • Proof for your donors. The drop-center ledger and the delivery receipt show that goods reached families. Donors can follow a single donation.
  • A trust tier you earn. Being vouched for by a known organization is visible to everyone, including the district office and international partners looking for local implementers.
  • One-door made easier. When the filing feature ships, the permission trail you must keep today by letter becomes a record you already have.

Government offices: DEOC, palikas and ward offices

  • The unreached list. The coverage page shows which wards under your responsibility have received nothing, and for how long, from all organizations at once.
  • A feed you can read every hour. Public, no login, in JSON or CSV, keyed to BIPAD's own ward ids. It complements BIPAD, which records incidents and losses, with what BIPAD does not hold: requests, handlers and deliveries.
  • The DDMC daily log, generated. Once distribution filing ships, the log the one-door policy requires is produced from what organizations already entered.

People in affected communities

  • Ask for help without an account, in Nepali, and get a reference code to check on it.
  • Know what is happening. When someone takes the request, hands it back or delivers, the status page says so.
  • Privacy by default. Names are masked in public. Phone numbers are seen only by the verified handler, and every such view is logged.

WORKING TOGETHER

How we handle data, and what we ask of you

Our commitments

  • Nothing is published until a person has verified it. Every moderator action is in a public audit log.
  • A beneficiary's full name and phone number are shown only to the moderator and to the organization or helper currently handling the request. Each view is recorded.
  • Public exports carry organization names and role labels, never a person's name or contact.
  • Data is yours to take. Exports are in CSV, HXL and GeoJSON, and the ward codes are the government's.
  • The platform handles no money and is not a government website. It does not replace the one-door system; it gives you the record to comply with it.
  • No automated matching. A person decides who helps whom.

What we ask

  1. Register your organization at verifiednepal.com/register-organization and tell us which districts and wards you work in.
  2. Use the take and deliver flow for the requests you fulfil, even when you would have helped anyway. That is what puts your work on the coverage page and in the 3W.
  3. Give us one information contact. One person who can tell us whether the export columns fit your reporting and what is missing.
  4. Point a desk at the feed. If you run an emergency operations centre or an information management unit, pull the coverage feed on a schedule and tell us what would make it more useful.

What we ask specifically of government partners

A DEOC or palika that reads the coverage page during the next monsoon, and tells us whether the DDMC log we generate matches what the one-door policy needs, would shape the platform more than any other single input.

Where the platform is heading. A national register of requests, handlers and deliveries that any organization can write to and any office can read, kept in use through every monsoon so it is already the habit when a large earthquake comes. The work is open source and the record is public. We would rather build it with you than for you.
